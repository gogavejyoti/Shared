Executive summary

The data shows a major operational transition between January and June 2026.

Handled volume peaked at 55,986 in April and fell to 40,554 in June, a 27.6% decline from peak. Delivered hours fell much faster, down 51.7% from January to June. As a result, reported productivity improved sharply from 2.50 to 4.33 contacts per hour, while total AHT declined from 24.0 to 13.9 minutes.

The main concern is that this productivity improvement is not purely demand-driven. The data suggests a combination of channel mix migration, reduced staffing or delivered capacity, and possible measurement or allocation changes. Schedule adherence and conformance remain weak, especially for CX and during Wk21 and Wk24.

The strongest management signal is this

Capacity is being removed faster than demand is falling, while scheduling quality remains inconsistent.

That creates a risk of hidden service degradation if volume rebounds or if the reported productivity gains are partly caused by under-recorded hours or work shifting between channels.

Top 10 hidden insights

1. Delivered capacity is contracting much faster than demand

From January to June

  • Handled volume decreased by 16.3%
  • Delivered hours decreased by 51.7%
  • CPH increased by 73.3%
  • AHT decreased by 42.3%

The hours trend is statistically stronger than the volume trend. Delivered hours show a linear trend with p = 0.011, while volume has p = 0.259. This indicates that the dominant change is capacity or measurement-related rather than a clean demand decline.

2. April was the demand peak, but June is the operational stress point

April had the highest total handled volume at 55,986, but June had the lowest delivered hours at 9,369.

June therefore combines

  • Relatively low demand
  • Extremely low capacity
  • Highest CPH
  • Lowest AHT

This may look positive in a productivity report, but it is also the month most likely to contain hidden risk from backlog suppression, deferred work, incomplete hour capture, or unsustainable workload compression.

3. Channel mix is moving away from voice toward digital work

Between January and June

  • Voice volume fell 47.0%
  • Chat volume increased 30.3%
  • Email volume fell 23.4%
  • Chat share increased from 14.1% to 21.9%
  • Voice share declined from 2.2% to 1.4%

This mix shift matters because each channel consumes labor differently. A simple total CPH comparison can overstate productivity if the workload is moving toward shorter or more standardized interactions.

4. CS is shrinking much faster than CX

CS total volume declined 44.5% from January to June, while CX total volume declined only 14.0%.

The largest segment-level contraction is CS email, down 51.7%. In contrast, CX International email increased 71.2%.

This suggests one of the following possibilities

  • Work migration between teams or regions
  • Different queue routing
  • A change in case ownership
  • Structural demand reduction in CS
  • Data capture differences between business units

The shift should be validated before using the results to reduce CS staffing.

5. CX International email is the clearest growth pocket

CX International email volume increased from 5,661 to 9,689, making it the strongest expanding workload in the dataset.

This creates a possible opportunity for

  • Dedicated international email specialization
  • Queue-specific automation
  • Reusable response libraries
  • Skill-based scheduling
  • Cross-training from lower-volume CS email work

The growth is large enough that it should not be treated as ordinary monthly noise.

6. Voice is volatile and labor-intensive

Voice has the highest volume coefficient of variation at 36.1%, compared with

  • Chat at 24.4%
  • Email at 10.9%
  • Total volume at 11.6%

Voice AHT also peaks at 64.4 minutes in May, while voice CPH falls to 0.93. This is a significant productivity deterioration and suggests a possible mix of complex contacts, low occupancy, staffing mismatch, or reporting inconsistency.

Voice needs separate forecasting and staffing logic. Total-volume forecasting alone will not protect the operation.

7. Productivity improved mostly because labor intensity fell

The total CPH increase is statistically significant at approximately p = 0.048, but total volume and total CPH are not significantly correlated over the six-month period.

This means the productivity increase cannot be confidently explained by higher or lower demand alone. It is more consistent with

  • Fewer hours being recorded
  • Work being moved to lower-effort channels
  • Less complex work remaining in the measured population
  • Staffing reductions
  • Process or system changes

The business should avoid celebrating the CPH gain until delivered-hour definitions and queue coverage are confirmed.

8. Schedule adherence is below a practical control threshold

Overall schedule adherence is below 80% in 7 of 12 weeks. The weakest week is Wk14 at 65.0%, while the best is Wk22 at 84.0%.

Overall conformance is even more unstable, reaching a low of 52.8% in Wk21 and 55.9% in Wk24.

Conformance is a more serious warning signal than adherence because it suggests people may be present but not following the required activity or schedule pattern.

9. CX has a persistent conformance weakness

CX conformance is below the other groups across most weeks, reaching only 55% in Wk17 and 56% in Wk18.

This is not just a single-week anomaly. It points toward a recurring operational issue such as

  • Incorrect activity coding
  • Poor schedule fit
  • Break and meeting leakage
  • Queue switching
  • Incomplete adherence tracking
  • Workforce management configuration problems

10. Headcount changes appear reactive rather than predictive

Overall headcount declines from 129 in Wk14 to 121 by Wk21, then jumps to 143 in Wk24 before settling at 141 in Wk25.

The jump occurs while adherence and conformance are still inconsistent. This suggests workforce actions may be reacting to operational pain rather than anticipating demand.

The data does not show a statistically reliable headcount-performance relationship, which means simply adding people may not solve the underlying scheduling or process problem.

Critical risks ranked by impact and likelihood

High impact and high likelihood

Capacity withdrawal exceeding sustainable demand decline

Delivered hours fell more than three times as fast as handled volume between January and June. If volume returns toward the April level, the operation may not have enough recorded capacity.

Schedule conformance failure

Conformance falls below 60% in several weeks, with the lowest point at 52.8%. This is a direct risk to service consistency, staffing efficiency, and forecast reliability.

Data-definition or allocation risk

The sharp CPH improvement combined with falling hours creates a credible possibility that work, hours, or queue ownership changed. Without validation, staffing decisions could be based on misleading productivity gains.

Medium-high impact and medium likelihood

Voice under-capacity during complex-contact periods

Voice AHT reaches 64.4 minutes in May while voice CPH falls below 1.0. A return of voice demand could create rapid backlog or service-level deterioration.

CS structural underutilization or work migration

CS volume declines 44.5%, driven largely by email. If work migrated elsewhere, CS staffing may be misaligned rather than genuinely unnecessary.

International email concentration risk

CX International email grows 71.2%. This creates a single-channel growth dependency and could become a bottleneck if specialist coverage is limited.

Medium impact and medium likelihood

Reactive headcount swings

The sudden increase from 121 to 143 scheduled headcount suggests unstable staffing decisions and potential overstaffing in some periods alongside understaffing in others.

Forecasting weakness

There is no explicit forecast, plan, required hours, backlog, service level, or shrinkage field. Forecast accuracy cannot be measured directly.

Data quality and analytical limitations

The workbook is usable, but several issues reduce confidence in direct forecasting.

No explicit forecast field

Forecast accuracy cannot be calculated because the file does not contain forecast volume, forecast hours, required staffing, backlog, service level, or forecast error.

Short time series

There are only six monthly observations from January through June 2026. This is not enough to establish true seasonality. The apparent pattern should be treated as a structural trend hypothesis rather than confirmed seasonality.

Schedule data is not date-aligned to monthly operations

The schedule sheet contains Wk14 to Wk25, but there is no explicit mapping between these weeks and the January to June monthly records. Cross-period causal analysis should therefore be limited.

Potential terminology and naming issues

The workbook uses truncated or inconsistent labels such as Conformanc, CX Intl, and the apparent source typo Adherece. These do not affect the calculations directly, but they increase the risk of joining or reporting errors.

No clear explanation for the June change

June contains several simultaneous extremes. It should be flagged for source-system and operational validation before being used as a forward planning baseline.

Unexpected findings valuable for decision-making

The most unexpected finding is that the operation appears more productive exactly when its recorded capacity is collapsing. That is usually not a normal business pattern unless there has been a major process improvement, channel migration, staffing change, or data-definition change.

The second unexpected finding is that the largest operational weakness is not total volume. It is the combination of unstable conformance, channel-specific volatility, and uncertain delivered-hour measurement.

The practical conclusion is

Do not optimize for higher CPH until the business confirms that lower delivered hours represent genuine efficiency rather than missing capacity or missing data.

The most urgent next step is to reconcile June delivered hours and validate the movement of CS email work into CX, CX International, or unmeasured queues.
